\section{Methodology}
\subsection{Overview}
We implemented a spindle architecture for Chinese historical text recognition called SpindleNet. 
The overview framework is shown in Fig.\ref{fig:overall}. The architecture consists of three modules: a feature extraction module, a character length and weight prediction module, and a decoder module. The input image is first pre-processed by scaling its width to 32 pixels keeping the aspect ratio and then center padding into a 32x320 image $\Nimage$. 

The image is then passed to the feature extraction network$\NFE$, resulting output feature maps $\Nfeatmap$: $(\Nfeatmapat{1}, ... ,\Nfeatmapat{5})$, 
\begin{equation}
    \Nfeatmap=\NFE(\Nimage).
\end{equation}
The fifth layer is then encoded in to the \Tefinalfeat{} $\Nfinalfeat{}$ with a convolution block,
\begin{equation}
    \Nfinalfeat=Conv(\Nfeatmapat{5}).
\end{equation}

Features from the first and the third of the feature extraction network, together with the \Tefinalfeat{}, are input to an attention module to predict the sequence length $\Nlenpred$ and the location masked of each individual character $\Nattmap$,
\begin{equation}
    \Nattmap,\Nlenpred=\NCAM(\Nfeatmapat{1}, \Nfeatmapat{3}, \Nfinalfeat{})
\end{equation}

The image features are then sampled into time-stamp aligned character features $\Nalignedfeat$, 
\begin{equation}
    \Nalignedfeatat{t}=\sum_{i,j}^{w,h} \Nattmap_{t,i,j}\Nfinalfeat_{i,j}
\end{equation}

The character features are then input to the decoder for prediction. 
\begin{equation}
    \Npred=\NPRED(\Nalignedfeat).
\end{equation}
For more details, please refer to vsdf\cite{vsdf}.

\subsection{Feature extraction network}

The feature extraction network consists of multiple ResNet layers. Traditional feature extraction networks have multiple output channels at each layer, with the number of channels increasing for deeper layers. This forms a hierarchical increasing structure, which we denote as a triangle structure. This structure is used in the baseline method. We found that the triangle structure has limited performances on historical document text recognition tasks. Our research show that one factor limits the perfomances is the imbalanced nature of the historical document text dataset.
The triangle structure yields poor performance for tail classes. 

In this work, we propose to emphasis the component level feature representation, as features at this level are shared by head classes and tail classes alike, providing more generalization capability~\cite{sanicdar23,fudanvi}.
Since each character is composed by several components, learning at this level is less prone to overfitting, hence can also increase the head class performance as well.

As the implementation, we propose to simply allocate more channels to the corresponding layers. Due to the high frequency of characters, character components should match ``pattern''~\cite{dissection} level features, which is mostly modeled by the second and the third layer of the network.
Hence, in this work, we allocate the parameter mainly to the third layer, yielding a spindle structure, yielding the spindle network.
%Inspired by MobileNet, we experimented with various structures for the feature extraction network, such as a reversed triangle structure, in which the shallow layers have more channels and the deep layers have fewer channels. We also tried a structure with more intermediate layers and fewer layers on the edges. We finally found that the best overall performance is achieved when the number of channels in the intermediate layers is increased, and the best performance for tail classes is also achieved.

The baseline network structure follows a triangular configuration, in which the number of channels gradually increases as the network extends into deeper layers. By contrast, the spindle-shaped network structure follows a reversed triangular design that reduces the number of channels as the depth increases. Specifically, the baseline comprises five ResNet layers with channel numbers set to [32, 64, 64, 128, 256], while the spindle-shaped counterpart also incorporates five ResNet layers but allocates channels as [32, 64, 256, 128, 64]. Their configurations are clearly depicted in Fig.\ref{fig:base_and_spindle} for direct comparison.

The baseline architecture is frequently adopted in generic image classification tasks, as it offers a straightforward approach that incrementally broadens its representational capacity in deeper layers. However, when applied to the recognition of ancient document text, it yields suboptimal performance. A significant factor behind this limitation is the highly imbalanced nature of ancient text datasets, which include large sets of rare and infrequently occurring characters. The baseline network, while effective for balanced data, struggles to capture and retain the defining features of uncommon symbols due to insufficient capacity in the layers that matter most for nuanced feature learning. Consequently, its ability to model rare characters diminishes, resulting in inferior recognition outcomes for those classes.

 On the other hand, the spindle-shaped network structure tackles this imbalance by strategically enlarging the number of channels in the intermediate layers. This design choice amplifies the representational power precisely where it can capture more discriminative details. In ancient documents, many characters share subtle strokes or radical components that demand finer layers of differentiation, especially among scarce symbols. By expanding the channel capacity at the middle depth, the network gains a richer pool of learned features, which significantly benefits the depiction of uncommon shapes, strokes, and structural nuances. To preserve overall parameter efficiency, the number of channels then narrows in the deeper layers, creating the reversed triangular profile.

Empirical evaluations indicate that the spindle-shaped network structure achieves superior results on ancient document text recognition tasks, surpassing the baseline model by maintaining high accuracy on both frequently seen characters and those that appear sporadically. Its unique “spindle” design more effectively addresses the challenge of imbalance and rarity, as it allows for broader, more diverse feature learning in the portion of the network that influences character representation the most. Extensive experimental findings confirm that this architecture not only delivers robust overall accuracy but also excels specifically in recognizing rare characters, a crucial requirement for historical text analysis. By offering enhanced feature extraction capabilities in the intermediate layers, the spindle-shaped structure embodies a promising solution for dealing with ancient document datasets that exhibit considerable imbalance.

\subsection{Radical Residual Module}

Inspired by the Convolutional Block Attention Module (CBAM)\cite{woo2018cbam}, we employ a similar mechanism, referred to here as the Radical Residual Module (RRM), to capture richer radical information in subsequent classification tasks. This RRM is designed as a hybrid attention mechanism that integrates both spatial and channel attentions into a single unified framework. By fusing these two types of attention, the module selectively focuses on the most salient radical patterns within the feature maps, thereby highlighting crucial elements necessary for robust classification. This approach is grounded in the principle that linguistic radicals often carry essential semantic or structural cues, so emphasizing them can lead to significant performance gains in tasks that rely on detailed textual or symbolic information.

More concretely, given an intermediate feature map as input, the RRM first infers a 1D channel attention map and then proceeds to infer a 2D spatial attention map. During this sequential attention inference, the module explicitly reweights channel responses in order to highlight channels that capture discriminative radical features, and next it refines the spatial distribution of these features by focusing on the specific locations where radicals are most relevant. This dual attention scheme can be seen as an extension of previous attention-based architectures, which have proven effective in various tasks by adaptively emphasizing pertinent features\cite{hu2018squeeze,vaswani2017attention}. As a result, the RRM ensures that each part of the feature map receives an importance weight reflecting both channel-level and spatial-level significance.

The overall attention process can be summarized mathematically as
\[
\mathbf{F}' = \mathbf{F} \otimes \mathbf{M}_\text{ch} \otimes \mathbf{M}_\text{sp},
\]
where \(\mathbf{F}\) denotes the input feature map, \(\mathbf{M}_\text{ch}\) the 1D channel attention map, and \(\mathbf{M}_\text{sp}\) the 2D spatial attention map. The symbol \(\otimes\) represents element-wise multiplication. In the multiplication process, attention values are appropriately broadcast or replicated: channel attention values are duplicated across the spatial dimensions, and the spatial attention values are extended across the channel dimension. This broadcasting strategy allows the network to integrate each attention map seamlessly, ensuring that the final reweighted feature map \(\mathbf{F}'\) captures the most meaningful radical cues while discarding less informative signals.

Such a focus on radicals is beneficial when the classification task relies on intricate or subtle components of characters or symbols, because it amplifies the crucial parts of the input while downplaying irrelevant details. As attention mechanisms continue to advance and demonstrate remarkable success in domains such as image recognition and natural language processing, designing a targeted module like the RRM capitalizes on their strengths and addresses domain-specific needs in radical-based recognition tasks. This synergy of channel and spatial attention has the potential to improve overall accuracy, increase robustness to noise, and provide deeper insights into how critical features are emphasized within the model.
