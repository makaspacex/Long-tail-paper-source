In this work, we rigorously evaluate our model on two major datasets containing ancient Buddhist scriptures, namely the Tripitaka Koreana in Han (TKH) Dataset and the Multiple Tripitaka in Han (MTH) Dataset~\cite{tkhmth}. These datasets capture a wealth of historical textual content, encompassing a broad spectrum of Chinese characters and stylistic variations. The richness and diversity present in these corpora make them particularly well-suited for testing the robustness and generalization capabilities of the proposed method. Following the standard approach outlined in~\cite{jla}, we employ the combined version of TKH and MTH2200, commonly referred to as MTHv2. In this paper, we specifically rely on TKHMTH2200 because TKHMTH2200 consists of TKH, MTH1000, and MTH1200, thereby providing a comprehensive range of traditional text samples with considerable diversity in character set and layout. The MTHv2 dataset offers multiple levels of annotations, including line-level annotation, character-level annotation, and “boundary lines” with corresponding reading order information, all of which are integral to a deeper understanding and accurate recognition of ancient texts. Despite these additional annotations being available, we primarily utilize the line-level annotations in this study, given that they require minimal overhead yet ensure a reliable ground truth to guide the training process.

Protocol-wise, we measure the overall performance of our approach on the complete testing set in strict accordance with the data split from~\cite{jla}. The training and testing subsets of MTHv2 were originally partitioned at a ratio of 3:1, and to maintain consistency with established benchmarks, we adhere to the exact same division. Importantly, our training and test data remain identical to the setup used by~\cite{jla}, ensuring that any differences in performance can be ascribed to our method rather than variations in the dataset split. Additionally, no external resources such as synthetic data or pretrained models are introduced at any stage, eliminating confounding factors that could otherwise skew our comparisons. Beyond the primary benchmarking experiments, we also conduct thorough ablation studies and behavioral analyses to validate the efficacy of our technique and clarify the contribution of key components.

For the open-set test protocol, we incorporate a broader assessment strategy to capture the model’s ability to handle new or previously unseen characters. When a text line contains unfamiliar symbols, that text line is evaluated in terms of AR, CR, and LA. The ACC indicator thus reflects the recognition accuracy specifically for these novel characters, providing a straightforward gauge of open-set performance. Conversely, the close-set test adopts a more conventional standard in which the presence or absence of new characters is not explicitly differentiated. This dual testing methodology grants insight into both the classic close-set scenario and the more challenging open-set scenario, underscoring our model’s versatility and robustness in dealing with rare or unknown classes.

 \subsection{Implementation details}
 Our implementation is grounded in the OpenCCD code base~\cite{vsdf}, offering a comprehensive and well-organized framework for handling ancient text recognition tasks. To ensure consistency in data pre-processing, we resize the input image to a height of $32$ pixels and then center-pad the image to the final dimension of $32\times 320$. This choice not only standardizes the input but also provides adequate space for accommodating varying text lengths. The network is trained from scratch for a total of $128$ epochs with a batch size of $64$, which strikes a practical balance between efficient parallelization and reliable gradient estimation.

All experiments are conducted on a virtual machine configured with Pytorch-$1.12.1$, TorchVision-$0.13.1$, CUDA-$11.2$, and Ubuntu $22.04$. This software stack ensures compatibility and optimal performance for deep learning workloads. When employing a single Nvidia RTX 4090 GPU, completing $128$ training epochs typically takes around $10$ hours, indicating that the workflow is both computationally intensive and adequately scalable to large datasets. We observe that GPU memory usage ranges between $11$GB and $21$GB, depending on the specific model variant. Such variance in memory footprint highlights the inherent trade-off between model complexity and resource consumption.

To promote transparency and reproducibility, we publicly release our models, code, and corresponding documentation on~\url{https://github.com/makaspacex/spindlenet}. By providing open access to these resources, we encourage the research community to replicate our experiments, extend our methods, and facilitate further innovations in ancient document recognition.

\subsubsection{Per-class Performance Analysis} \input{figures/freqvsimprovement.tex} \noindent To gain deeper insights into how the proposed model improves recognition results on both common and infrequently occurring characters, this section examines the per-class performance changes. The overall trend is presented in Fig.\ref{fig:freqvsimprovement}, which illustrates how character frequency correlates with the degree of accuracy improvement and intra-class variance reduction. For clarity, the character frequency is derived from the entire dataset because new characters do not appear in the training set. If we limited the statistics solely to training data, newly introduced characters would not be represented in these calculations, so incorporating the entire corpus ensures a more comprehensive portrait of how often each character category occurs.

The blue line in Fig.\ref{fig:freqvsimprovement} is arranged in descending order of character frequency, systematically capturing the range from head to tail classes. In order to align these frequency-based categories with the performance metrics, the red line signifies two complementary indicators: the improvement in recognition accuracy and the corresponding reduction in intra-class variance. Given that the raw data exhibits substantial fluctuations, direct visualization of the trend can be noisy. Hence, the red line is smoothed via the moving average method using a window size of 250, which unveils the underlying pattern more clearly by minimizing local volatility.

From the smoothed data, it is apparent that the head class (consisting of frequent characters) experiences a relatively modest improvement in both accuracy and stability. By contrast, the tail class benefits from a notably larger gain in performance, suggesting that the proposed SpindleNet excels at capturing subtle features of rare and novel characters. Such improvements are particularly significant in ancient text recognition tasks, where a considerable fraction of characters appear only infrequently and exhibit complex variations. The boosted performance for the tail class underlines SpindleNet’s enhanced ability to learn robust feature representations for low-frequency categories, which directly translates to stronger recognition of new or unseen symbols.

In summary, these findings confirm that SpindleNet not only preserves performance on common characters but also markedly elevates the accuracy of rare classes, including characters that fall entirely outside the training set. By effectively narrowing intra-class variance and lifting recognition rates for underrepresented symbols, SpindleNet demonstrates its capability to better capture the intricate features critical for handling the broad range of characters found in ancient documents. The subsequent section will present detailed quantitative results of these novel character evaluations, further highlighting the model’s adaptability and strength in managing open-set text recognition.

\subsubsection{Novel Characters Performance on MTHv2} 
\input{tables/zero_shot.tex} 
In order to comprehensively evaluate the proposed model’s ability to recognize previously unseen characters, we analyze the performance on text samples that explicitly include such new symbols, as summarized in Table~\ref{tab:zero_shot}. These samples are particularly challenging because they contain characters that the model never encountered during training, thus demanding robust feature representation and generalization. Specifically, our base model attains an accuracy of \textbf{51.40} in recognizing new characters, whereas the improved spindle network reaches a higher accuracy of \textbf{52.96}. It is noteworthy that this accuracy for new characters is computed under more stringent criteria. Unlike other evaluations where alignment or post-processing steps might boost results, here we refrain from any alignment adjustments prior to scoring. Only when the model’s predicted characters exactly match the ground-truth (GT) characters at their respective positions are they deemed correct. This stringent procedure allows us to isolate genuine performance gains and highlights the model’s raw predictive strength.

Following this strict regime, whenever a line of text contains previously unseen characters, we incorporate that line into the calculations for $AR$, $CR$, and $LA$. This approach gives us a well-defined perspective on how effectively the model handles genuine out-of-vocabulary symbols in a real-world recognition scenario. Observing Table~\ref{tab:zero_shot}, it is evident that SpindleNet achieves state-of-the-art (SOTA) results across all relevant metrics, including $AR$, $CR$, $ACC$, and $LA$. These superior outcomes underscore the spindle architecture’s capacity to capture essential features of unfamiliar glyphs that do not appear in the training data. Coupled with the earlier findings regarding tail-class performance, this reinforces our assertion that novel characters in the test set align closely with the tail-end distribution, and that strengthening feature representations for infrequent classes inherently aids in recognizing unseen symbols. Collectively, these results confirm that SpindleNet successfully bridges the gap between conventional closed-set scenarios and more open-set or unseen character cases, making it a robust solution for historical texts containing extensive and sparsely sampled character inventories.


\subsection{Close-set Comparison with SOTA}
 \input{figures/tsne.tex} 
\noindent While the open-set perspective provides insight into the handling of unseen characters, it is also crucial to assess how the proposed model performs under conventional close-set conditions where all classes in the test set are previously observed during training. Table 2 reports the performance of our model on widely adopted recognition indicators, revealing that our method achieves SOTA in each metric for the closed-set evaluation. In particular, the values for $AR$, $CR$, and $LA$ stand at 94.21, 95.27, and 70.77, respectively. These figures indicate that the architecture maintains strong recognition capabilities even for classes that are less rare or have been seen before, in addition to its notable gains for tail classes.

From a deeper examination, as also indicated in Fig.\ref{fig:freqvsimprovement}, the performance gains predominantly stem from improved handling of tail classes. By allocating a substantial representational capacity within the intermediate layers, SpindleNet manages to reduce confusion and misclassifications for low-frequency symbols, thereby boosting the aggregate accuracy. Beyond mere numerical performance, it is instructive to examine how the learned features differ in our proposed model compared to the baseline. Hence, we conduct a qualitative analysis with an emphasis on the distribution of character embeddings.

\input{tables/sota} 
Fig.\ref{fig:tsne} illustrates a t-SNE visualization (generated via the tsne-cuda tool) of the final feature extractor layer’s embeddings. Clearly, SpindleNet produces clusters that exhibit sharper and more coherent boundaries, enabling a more distinct separation among different classes of characters. One illustrative example involves a group of orange-colored characters that, in the baseline approach, end up divided into two separate regions, indicating inconsistent or conflicting representations. In contrast, SpindleNet consolidates these characters into a compact cluster, revealing that they share fundamental visual patterns. This cohesive grouping attests to SpindleNet’s enhanced discriminative power and feature extraction capabilities, which not only yield higher accuracy but also offer better stability and robustness when dealing with closely related or visually similar symbols.

After a sufficient period of training, SpindleNet consistently outperforms the baseline model across all measured metrics. This outcome indicates that the newly introduced modules and design refinements equip the network with stronger feature extraction capabilities and improved training stability. In particular, the capacity to extract more discriminative local and global features helps SpindleNet better encode and differentiate among the various components and radicals comprising each character. As a result, it becomes more adept at building associations between these constituent parts, fostering a deeper understanding of both frequent and rare characters. Consequently, this leads to notable improvements in accuracy for challenging tail-class characters and even enables the model to handle previously unseen characters more effectively. These findings underscore how targeted enhancements to the architecture can yield appreciable gains in both performance and resilience when dealing with diverse and imbalanced ancient text datasets.

\subsubsection{Architecture-level Ablative}
\input{figures/archabl}

Beyond examining specific modules, it is also essential to evaluate the broader architectural design to determine where and how additional parameters should be allocated for maximal impact. This perspective sheds light on structural sensitivity, highlighting which convolutional layers benefit most from increased channel capacity. As shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:archabl}, we analyze performance variations under two contrasting extremes. On the one hand, there is the equilateral triangle structure employed by the baseline model, which steadily increases the channel size from shallower to deeper layers. On the other hand, there is the inverted triangle structure, where deeper layers have fewer channels.

The results reveal that our SpindleNet design outperforms these two extremes, reinforcing the idea that strategically increasing the number of channels in the head or intermediate layers can better capture the complex features of ancient characters. By carefully distributing channel resources in this spindle-like arrangement—broadening mid-layers to accommodate richer feature learning and tapering off toward the deeper layers—SpindleNet attains higher representational flexibility without imposing an unnecessarily large parameter burden on the later stages. This nuanced allocation proves especially advantageous for rare or novel characters, where subtle visual distinctions demand more powerful feature extraction. Altogether, these architectural-level insights emphasize the importance of placing additional network capacity at layers that have the greatest influence on feature differentiation, culminating in a more robust and effective solution for the unique challenges posed by ancient document recognition.
